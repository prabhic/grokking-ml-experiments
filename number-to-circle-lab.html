Unit circle · phase · sine and cosine

A number can become a point that is easy to rotate.

The unit circle turns an angle into two coordinates: cosine on the horizontal axis and sine on the vertical axis. This small trick lets later systems work with angles using ordinary vectors.

One number, two coordinates

The angle is one number. The point is [cos θ, sin θ]. Every point on the unit circle has length 1.

Why this is useful

Nearby angles become nearby points. Rotation becomes a change in coordinates. Dot products can compare directions, and the fixed length keeps scale controlled.

From circle to wave

If the angle increases as position increases, the x and y coordinates rise and fall smoothly. Those changing coordinates are the sine and cosine waves used by positional encoding.

History

The circle and trigonometric ideas have ancient roots across Babylonian, Greek, Indian, and Islamic mathematics; there is no single inventor of the unit-circle representation. Hipparchus is often credited with early Greek chord tables. The Transformer’s sinusoidal positional encoding was introduced by Vaswani and colleagues in Attention Is All You Need (2017).

Terms

phase
An angle or position in a repeating cycle.
unit circle
A circle with radius 1.
coordinate
One component of the point: cosine or sine.
