LLM first principles · Activation Function Playground

An activation decides how much of each number passes through.

Inside the feed-forward network, every hidden value meets one small function. ReLU cuts negatives to zero. GELU and SiLU let small negatives leak through a little. SwiGLU multiplies SiLU by a second projection. This playground shows each curve, its slope, and the function acting on a whole hidden vector at once.

Read the playground

The left plot draws both functions with the selected one bold. The dot is the current input; the short tangent is its gradient. The right side is one hidden vector of the feed-forward network: the first element is your slider, the rest are fixed. Each element goes through the same function independently.

What this isolates

Only the nonlinearity. No weights change here. The feed-forward lab adds the two linear layers on either side of this function.

Why it matters

Two linear layers in a row collapse into one linear layer. The activation between them is what lets the feed-forward network represent anything more than a single matrix multiply. Its gradient also decides whether a hidden unit can keep learning: a ReLU unit stuck below zero receives no gradient at all.

Terms

elementwise means the function is applied to each number on its own. Φ(x) is the standard normal cumulative distribution, the chance a random normal draw is below x. dead unit is a ReLU input that stays negative, so its output and gradient are both zero.

Try this

  1. Drag x below zero with ReLU selected and watch both readouts go to exactly 0.
  2. Switch to GELU at the same x: the output is slightly negative and the gradient is not zero.
  3. Move x past 2 and see the two curves become nearly identical.
  4. Find the x where GELU is most negative (about −0.75) and read its value.
  5. Select SiLU and watch a positive hidden value become negative because its value projection is negative.

A short history

Rectifier-style units go back to Fukushima's 1969 and 1980 work, but ReLU became the default after Nair and Hinton (2010) and Glorot, Bordes, and Bengio's 2011 deep sparse rectifier paper showed it trained deep networks faster than sigmoids. GELU was proposed by Hendrycks and Gimpel in 2016 and adopted by BERT and GPT-2; the tanh approximation used here is the one GPT-2 ships. SiLU, also called Swish, was studied by Elfwing and colleagues (2017) and by Ramachandran, Zoph, and Le (2017); Shazeer (2020) combined it with a multiplicative gate as SwiGLU, now the default in LLaMA, Mistral, and Gemma. Sigmoid and tanh, the earlier defaults, saturate on both sides and starve deep networks of gradient, which is why they left the feed-forward layer.
