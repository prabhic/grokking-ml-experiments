2 predict
Run the two knobs forward: ŷ = m·x + b. That's the model's entire opinion about this house.
3 compare
The miss is y − ŷ. Positive → point is above the line → the line must come up. Negative → it must come down. The sign of the miss IS the direction.
4 nudge
Both knobs move a fraction h toward the point: b gets h·miss (translate), m gets h·x·miss (rotate). Nothing else in the model exists to change.

Linear regression · the square trick · gradient descent

The line doesn't see the pattern. It gets nudged into it.

A model here is just two knobs: ŷ = m·x + b — slope m (price per room) and intercept b (base price). Training never solves anything: it picks one house, measures the miss, and nudges both knobs a tiny amount toward that house. The same tiny nudge, repeated thousands of times, rolls the line down an error mountain. Every dot on that mountain is a line on the plot above.

Read the playground

Each dot is a house: rooms on x, price on y. The dark line is the current model. Its only moving parts are slope m and intercept b. Dashed drops show each miss; the pink areas make the square error literal.

RMSE is the score to make small. It means the model misses a house by roughly that many price units. The blue ◆ is different: it is the model's prediction for a four-room house, which should approach 300 as the fit improves.

One nudge per step

Pick a house, predict, measure the miss, nudge both knobs. That's the whole algorithm — the square trick:

miss = y − ŷ
b ← b + h · miss
m ← m + h · x · miss

Why this collapses the book's four rotate/translate cases into one: the miss carries its own sign. Point above the line → miss positive → both knobs go up. Below → negative → both go down. And multiplying by x makes the rotation stronger for far-out points — exactly what a lever arm should do.

Two pictures, one model

The plot on the left and the mountain on the right are the same object seen twice. A line in data space = a single dot in (m, b) space. A good fit = a low point on the mountain.

Here is the honest part: training never sees the mountain. No map, no global view. Each step it feels the local slope through one house's miss and takes one small step downhill. That blind local rule finding the valley anyway — that is gradient descent, and it's the same principle that trains networks with billions of knobs instead of two.

What the top bar changes

Nudge size h is the learning rate: too small crawls; too large overshoots. Square makes big misses produce big nudges and a smooth error bowl. Absolute uses only the miss's sign, so every nudge has a fixed size and the landscape has a sharp bottom.

Steer lets you become the optimizer: ↑ ↓ translate b, ← → rotate m, Shift makes ten-times finer moves, and Esc stops. The speed slider affects the algorithm's narrated playback, not your keyboard stride.

The words on this page

feature · x
What we predict from — here, the number of rooms.
label · y
What we predict — the price. Data = (feature, label) pairs.
model · ŷ = m·x + b
A formula from feature to prediction. Two numbers, fully described.
weight · m
Slope: how much the prediction rises per extra room. Turning it rotates the line.
bias · b
Intercept: the prediction at x = 0. Turning it translates the line.
error function
One number scoring the whole line: sum (or mean) of misses — absolute or squared.
RMSE
√(mean of squared misses) — back in price units: the typical miss per house.
epoch
One pass through every house, one nudge each.
learning rate · h
Nudge size. Set by you, never learned.
gradient descent
Repeat "feel the local slope, step downhill" until the error stops falling.
parameters vs hyperparameters
What training changes (m, b) vs what you set before training (h, epochs, trick).
stochastic
Nudging on one point at a time — what this page does. Batch = compute on all points, then one nudge.

Try this

  1. Crank h to 0.35 and Train 100. The chart explodes — every nudge overshoots the valley and lands higher on the far wall. This is the classic "learning rate too big" failure.
  2. Drop h to 0.0001. The line still learns — at a crawl. Same destination, thousands more epochs.
  3. Switch to the Absolute trick and look at the mountain: the smooth bowl becomes a sharp cone, and near the bottom the line jitters forever — constant-size steps can't settle. That's why the square error's "nicer derivative" matters.
  4. Add an outlier, train, and compare tricks: the square trick drags the whole line toward the absurd house far more than the absolute trick does. Squaring makes big misses scream.
  5. Click a far corner of Mount Errorest, then Train 1000 — the trail rolls into the valley from anywhere. Different start, same bottom.
  6. Random line, then 🎮 Steer it yourself to the lowest RMSE you can. Then Train 1000 and see what the blind nudge rule beats you with.
